Fundamentals

What exactly is LPG?

Liquefied Petroleum Gas (LPG) is the fuel inside the cylinder connected to most Indian kitchen stoves. Understanding its basic properties is the first step to understanding why it's dangerous when it escapes.

01

Composition

Mainly a mixture of propane and butane, stored under pressure as a liquid inside the cylinder and released as a gas at the regulator.

02

Naturally odourless

Pure LPG has almost no smell. Suppliers deliberately add ethyl mercaptan, a strong "rotten egg" smelling compound, purely so leaks can be noticed.

03

Heavier than air

LPG vapour is denser than air, so a leak doesn't disperse upward — it sinks and pools near the floor, under cabinets, and in drains.

Root Causes

How does a gas leak actually happen?

Leaks are rarely sudden — they usually build up from ordinary wear that goes unnoticed for months.

Perished rubber tubing

The rubber hose connecting the regulator to the stove hardens and cracks with age and heat exposure — the single most common cause.

Worn or loose regulator

A regulator that isn't seated properly, or has aged rubber seals, allows gas to escape at the cylinder connection point.

Corroded cylinder valve

Rust or damage on the cylinder valve threading prevents a tight seal with the regulator.

Overfilled or damaged cylinders

Cylinders filled beyond safe capacity, or dented/damaged in transport, are more prone to leakage under pressure.

Loose stove-end fittings

Connections at the stove's gas inlet that are not properly clamped or tightened over time.

Expired safety components

Rubber hoses and regulators have a recommended replacement life (typically ~2 years for hoses) that many households don't track.

Why it's dangerous

The physics of how a small leak becomes a disaster

A leak by itself doesn't explode — it needs a specific sequence of conditions. Understanding this sequence is exactly what a detection system like GasGuard is designed to interrupt.

Step-by-step: leak → explosion

1. Gas escapes and, being heavier than air, sinks and accumulates in low, poorly ventilated spaces rather than dispersing.

2. As concentration rises, the air-gas mixture enters its flammable range — too little gas won't burn, but too much also won't ignite; there's a specific window.

0%2.2% (Lower Explosive Limit)9.5% (Upper Explosive Limit)100%

3. Within that 2.2%–9.5% window, any ignition source — a spark from a switch, an open flame, even static electricity — can ignite the accumulated cloud instantly and violently, rather than burning gradually like a normal flame.

4. In a sealed room, this also creates a secondary risk before ignition even happens: LPG displaces oxygen, which can cause dizziness or asphyxiation in a confined, unventilated space.

Prevention

Safety checklist for every household

  • Replace the rubber gas hose every 2 years, even if it looks fine
  • Use only ISI-marked regulators, hoses, and stoves
  • Get your regulator and connections inspected annually by your distributor
  • Keep the kitchen ventilated — never seal it airtight
  • Never leave a lit stove unattended
  • Learn the smell of the added odorant so you recognize a leak instantly
  • If you smell gas: do not switch on/off any electrical switch or use a phone in that room
  • Shut the cylinder valve immediately and open doors/windows to ventilate
  • Evacuate the area, then call for help from outside
  • Never use a naked flame to "check" for a leak — use soap water on joints instead
